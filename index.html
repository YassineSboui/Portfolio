From idea to production

How I take a system from a constraint to a URL someone else can open — and where each stage shows up in the work.

Start from the constraint, not the feature list.

Before a screen exists I write down what has to stay true — a price the model must never invent, a tenant that must never leak, a score nobody may trace.

  • MRAYAQBuilt instead of paying for Shopify, so prices had to be enforced on the server.
  • Clinic AIClinics reply late and without a price; the assistant may only quote published ones.
  • Talent BridgeKeyword filters rank a title above a skill; the score had to explain itself.

Draw the boundaries before the screens.

I decide where each rule is enforced and how many times — then make the boundary the thing the rest of the code cannot go around.

  • Elise.StudioA request's tenant is checked four times, the last inside PostgreSQL.
  • Auto-ÉcoleSix modules reach each other through query interfaces, never through each other's tables.
  • MRAYAQLayered so that no endpoint lambda ever touches the DbContext.

Build across the stack, from component to column.

Most of my work crosses the whole height of the system: the component, the API behind it, the table under it.

  • NeoForm38 component types, 45 validation rules, and the Blockly layer that wires them.
  • Tachkilti137 API routes behind one installable app, in three languages.
  • NeoLibrary37 components whose state classes are pure functions of props and context.

Make the rules executable.

A rule I care about gets a test that fails when it breaks — a missing database policy, a physical padding value, an endpoint without authorization.

  • Elise.StudioA conformance test goes red the moment a table ships without a policy.
  • NeoLibrary585 tests with nothing mounted, including one that forbids physical padding.
  • Tachkilti132 test files, from unit tests to Playwright runs on a real browser.

Ship where it can be checked.

The work ends where someone else can open it: a live URL, a pipeline, a migration that runs on every build.

  • TachkiltiVercel and Neon, with migrations applied at build time.
  • Auto-ÉcoleFlutter web on Vercel, the Spring Boot API in Docker on Render, PostgreSQL on Neon.
  • TripMateAn installable app whose opened pages still load offline.

Decisions

Eight calls I would defend in a review. Open one for what I rejected, what I did, and what it cost.

Nobody knows who After every match, players score each other out of ten and can leave a note. The scores are only honest if nobody — organiser, platform owner, anyone holding the database — can see who gave what.
What I rejected
Storing the rater and hiding the column.
What I did
Every "who" column — rater, note sender, poll voter — holds only an HMAC keyed by a secret that never enters the database; notes and player ratings are also sealed with AES-256-GCM. A player finds their own ratings by recomputing the hash.
What it cost
The secret can never be rotated once ratings exist, and a moderator can hide a reported note but never learn who wrote it.
From the Tachkilti case study
SSRF filter Connectors have to reach customer systems — REST, SQL, storage and e-signature services we do not control.
What I rejected
Validating the URL.
What I did
A filter in the socket connect callback, refusing private-network targets on resolved IPs before any socket opens — closing the DNS-rebinding window.
What it cost
The check runs per connection, not once, and DISCOVERY_EGRESS_BLOCKED is a refusal a customer will occasionally see legitimately.
From the Elise.Studio case study
Publishing 0.524 The triage classifier's held-out accuracy is 0.894.
What I rejected
Shipping 0.894 alone.
What I did
Published 0.524 cross-source transfer recall beside it, in four places, because holding out an entire CDC/WHO source passage shows the classifier is largely learning one page's phrasing.
What it cost
Cholera reads 0.015, in public.
From the Symptom Triage Console case study
The 62 cap Keyword ATS filters rank a job title above a candidate who can do the work, and explain neither decision.
What I rejected
One confidence score.
What I did
Two stages of ceilings cap matches supported only by the job title: a title hit with a skills sub-score under 35 is held at 62.
What it cost
Real title matches get capped too.
From the Talent Bridge case study
Discard the reply The assistant answers foreign patients for Turkish clinics over WhatsApp, and must never quote a price it invented.
What I rejected
Correcting the number.
What I did
Every price the model writes is re-extracted against an allow-list built from the clinic's price list and FAQ; a reply carrying an unlisted figure is discarded whole, booking marker included.
What it cost
The patient gets a fixed "our coordinator will confirm your plan first" line instead of the answer they asked for, and the reply the model did write is discarded with it, booking included.
From the Clinic AI case study
Replay, don't store Instructors fill a session sheet after every driving lesson, the office can correct any sheet later, and the exam and billing modules read the result.
What I rejected
Keeping hours and skill levels as columns updated on save.
What I did
Nothing is stored twice: official hours, each skill's current level and the progress curve are replayed from the validated sheets on every read, so one corrected sheet changes them everywhere at once.
What it cost
Every read walks the student's whole history instead of reading one row.
From the Auto-École case study
Nothing mounted 37 components, an 8-state field spec, seven of those states resolved by a six-branch priority chain — disabled > readonly > invalid > warning > focused > default.
What I rejected
Rendering components to assert it.
What I did
Passthrough classes computed as pure functions of props and context, asserted in unit tests with nothing mounted, including a test that forbids physical padding so RTL stays correct.
What it cost
The tests assert classes, not pixels.
From the NeoLibrary case study
This page Ten demo films of real running product, across one site.
What I rejected
Autoplay and eager loading.
What I did
preload="none", posters sized to the box they sit in, one film decoding at a time, and a manual-pause flag that scrolling never overrides.
What it cost
A deliberate delay before a film starts. Measured on the first seven: 113 MB to 24 MB, scroll p99 99.7ms to 17ms, simultaneous loads 8 to 1, and a film you pause stays paused.
